1. The straight-line fit

Each dot is the average of a 30-second recording with a known weight on the cell. The solid line is the best straight line through them; the dashed part is that line continued to 250 kg, which is a guess until it has been measured.

Equation: output (V) = 0.0374 + 0.01856 × weight (kg). So the zero (nothing hanging) sits at 0.037 V, and force in newtons = (volts − 0.0374) × 528.35.

2. How far is each point from the line?

Straight-line fits can hide small bends, so here is the leftover for each point in kilograms. A bend would show up as a curve (a smile or a frown); a good fit looks like random scatter around zero.

  • Scatter is random, with no smile or frown. A curved fit did not improve on the straight line (the curve term is not distinguishable from zero).
  • The two 0 kg points are the furthest from the line (+0.11 and −0.18 kg). That is the zero wandering a little, not a scaling problem.
  • Slope between neighbouring weights stays between 18.3 and 19.1 mV/kg all the way (the highest value is the very first step, 0 to 2.5 kg).
  • Fitting only the weights up to 30 kg and then predicting 40, 50 and 60 kg misses by 0.01 to 0.18 kg.
The caveat that mattersThe data allow a slight bend. Within what you measured, the uncertainty left on a possible curve would move the reading at 250 kg by about ±4.3 kg (1 sigma). A good load cell is usually straight to better than 0.05 % of full scale, so a bend is unlikely, but nothing here proves it for the amplifier and cabling as a whole. That is what the heavy weights are for.

3. Other things the data show

The 50 Hz hum is large, but removable

The raw signal carries a 50 Hz ripple of about ±77 mV, which is ±4.2 kg on the scale. It is the same at every weight, so it is electrical pickup from the mains, not the weights moving. Averaging over a whole number of 20 ms periods removes it exactly. The app's 100 ms smoothing does that (the ripple falls from 87 mV to 12 mV standard deviation), and the 30 s means used here are not affected. A slower wobble of about 1 kg (2.5 times a second) is left in the smoothed line; it is also electrical, it averages out over a second or more, and it is small next to a 250 kg push.

Two consequences. First, always read the peak force from the smoothed signal; the highest raw sample can be about 4 kg too high. Second, because the ripple is that large it is worth fixing at the source when convenient: use a shielded, twisted cable for the amplifier output, connect the amplifier's signal ground to the DAQ's AI GND, and keep the cable away from mains leads and chargers.

What this says.
  • Anything that reaches 250 kg fixes the main problem: it cuts the uncertainty at 250 kg from about 3 to 7 kg down to under 1 kg. The exact choice of intermediate weights matters much less than reaching the top.
  • After that, the accuracy of the weights themselves is the limit. A 0.2 % error on 250 kg is 0.5 kg, which is more than the electrical scatter of the load cell (0.08 kg). Know your weights better than the 0.2 % level, because no regression can correct a weight that is wrongly labelled.
  • Only two weights (0 and 250) gives the best straight-line precision but cannot show whether it bends, so do not do that.

Recommended plan

Keep today's 0 to 60 kg data (it is good) and add the heavy end. If you can get the weights, it is also worth repeating the whole thing cleanly once.

  1. Warm up the amplifier for at least 30 minutes. Zero the app and record 10 s with nothing on the cell.
  2. Go up: 100, 150, 200, 250 kg. If possible add 75 and 125 kg. Hold each for 15 s (30 s is more than needed); ignore the first 2 s after loading.
  3. Come back down: 200, 100, then 0 kg. Differences between up and down show sticking in the cell (hysteresis).
  4. One check weight that is not in the fit, for example 125 kg or 35 kg, measured as the final step. The fitted line should predict it to within about 1 %.
  5. Finish with another no-load reading to see how far the zero moved during the session.

That is roughly 12 extra runs, about 15 to 20 minutes. Practical points for the weights:

  • Know the real mass. Use plates that have been weighed on a calibrated scale, or weigh the whole loaded assembly. Gym plates are often 1 to 2 % off their printed value. Write down the measured mass and use that in the fit.
  • Load the cell the same way as in the experiment (same strap or hook, same pull direction, vertical, no swinging).
  • Steps of 50 kg between 50 and 250 kg are practical with 25 kg plates and keep the number of changes small.
Check the cell's rated capacity before hanging 250 kgThe TAS501 is made in many capacities, including a 200 kg version. Read the label on your cell. 250 kg needs a cell rated at least 250 kg (a 300 kg or larger one is safer); the datasheet allows brief overload to 150 % but the calibration is only meaningful inside the rated range. Also confirm that the hook, strap and rig are rated for the load.

What else would be smart

  1. Use all points in the app's calibration. The app now uses the zero and one weight. A multi-point fit with a "check weight" step would use data like this directly and warn when the fit is poor. I can build that.
  2. A baseline in every trial (2 s of no load before each contraction) so the drift never reaches the result.
  3. Keep the strap geometry the same for calibration and testing, since a pull at an angle changes what the cell reads.
  4. Repeat the calibration at the start of each test day and after any change to wiring or amplifier gain; keep the file with the data.
  5. Gain: 250 kg gives about 4.7 V of the ±10 V input. That is comfortable, so there is no need to change the amplifier gain. The DAQ resolution (about 0.3 mV) is about 0.02 kg, far finer than the scatter.
